\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cha:conclusions}

This chapter aim to conclude the thesis with a short summary of our work, followed by
the list of the specific contributions we made; A general discussion about the achievements
and the applications of our algorithm will then follow, and finally we will conclude by acknowledging
the limitations and listing some potential future improvements.

\section{Summary}

In our work we developed a novel support structure 
optimization using genetic algorithms. As we explained in the motivations 
(\ref{cha:motivations}), some similar algorithms exist in the literature, but 
they were primarily focused on industrial 3D printing technologies such as SLS, 
whereas our algorithm is designed explicitly for FDM 3D printing (which is more 
common in the consumer space).

In order to apply genetic algorithms to FDM 3D printing support structure 
generation, two main challenges were solved: the first one was the evaluation 
of stiffness in a time efficient manner (which we tackled in chapter 
\ref{cha:stiffness_algo}), and the second one was the implementation of an 
algorithm that was able to generate a wide variety of support structures (and 
not only simple trees).

In the results chapter (\ref{cha:results}) we have shown how our algorithm is 
indeed able to save a significant amount of material compared to state of the 
art methods, with a few minor downsides. Some of these, such as the contact 
points structure being inferior to the state of the art one, are temporary and 
could be improved in future works, while others, such as the time necessary 
for the optimization or the longer print times, are structural to the method 
itself and are probably not improvable by as much.

Overall, our work has shown that there are still significant improvements in 
terms of material consumption that could be made to support structure 
generation algorithms, and it has also shown that genetic algorithms could be 
a viable approach to solve the problem.


\section{Contributions}
Our work presented four key contributions, of which the first two can be considered
``major'', while the last two are minor, but still significant.
The four contribution ordered by relevance are:

\begin{description}
\item[Monte Carlo stiffness evaluation] (Chapter~\ref{cha:stiffness_algo})
This algorithm made the entire project viable, as having a proxy for the stiffness
of the support structure was essential in the reduction of the z-wobble effect (\ref{sec:wobble-test}).
Algorithm for stiffness and flexibility evaluation of rigid frames already existed of course,
however, as we shown in section \ref{sec:stiff_speed_cmp}, using those would have resulted
in our optimization algorithm taking more than one day to execute, which is obviously not worth
doing to save 10-30 grams of plastic. By contrast, the algorithm we developed for stiffness
evaluation reduces the computational time from over one day to a few minutes, making the prospect
of saving those 10-30 grams a lot more enticing.

\item[Non-tree support structure optimization] (Section~\ref{sec:sup_struct_opt})
The previous methodologies for support structure optimization adopted
by Zhang et. al. 2020 \cite{ZHANG2020117} and Vaissier et. al. 2019 \cite{VAISSIER201911}
were limited to trees. however, as we shown in section 
\ref{sec:sup_type}, tree supports (at least if they have a constant beam width)
are unstable for supporting tall regions in the context of FDM 3D printing, 
as they tend to flex to much and potentially detaching fro the plate.
For this reason we developed a novel optimization algorithm that was able
to optimize a wider variety of support topology. This wider variety
of support topology was used to produce stiffer structure, that were indeed
able to reach and support taller critical areas.

\item[Improved critical/floating region detection] (Sections~\ref{sec:crit_detection}, \ref{sec:floating_regions_detection})
Our proposed solution payed special attention to floating regions, and to the ``propagation'' of critical
areas, resulting in a support structure generation that was more aware of what regions required to be simply
supported, and what regions required to be supported and to be provided with some stiffness.
Our procedure was proven essential with the ``Dragon'' print test, where using ``stock'' state of the
art method resulted in several failed prints forcing us to manually add some supports; meanwhile
our method was able to generate a feasible support structure without user input.


\item[Contact Points Grouping using CPPNs] (Section~\ref{sec:contact_points_grouping})
The grouping of the contact points is not strictly essential, however it is required
for the divide and conquer approach we used to speed up the support structure optimization.
Our approach for the grouping of nodes was based on a Compositional Pattern Producing
Network (CPPN) which, although is not a novel idea, can be considered novel in the specific
context of support structure optimization.


\end{description}

\section{Discussion}

Our work has shown that there are still significant improvement that could be made 
to support structure optimization algorithm for FDM 3D printing. In our implementation
we allowed for a more flexible topology of the support; specifically we used a rigid frame,
while all previous literature we considered were limited to tree-like structures.
This allowed to maintain sufficiently stiff structure without the need of increasing
the diameter of the beams (and thus consuming more material).
This need for a stiffer structure stemmed from the fact that our work is focused 
on FDM 3D printing, where the nozzle is applying some significant forces to the supports
while it is depositing the material.
Previous works (namely as Zhang et. al. 2020 and Vaissier et. al. 2019) did not considered
stiffness in their cost function as they were more focused on industrial technologies such as SLS, resulting
in their support being to compliant for an application in FDM 3D printing.
Our work has therefore ``bridged'' the gap between the research world (where genetic algorithm
have been used for support structure optimization, but only for industrial 3D printing technologies)
and the ``consumer 3D printer'' world (that is dominated by FDM printers, and where genetic algorithms
were never applied in the context of support structure optimization).

Our approach compared favorably with the best available algorithms in it's class 
as we have measured an average reduction of 43\% in material consumption.
For this benchmark we have used OrcaSlicer's ``Tree Supports'' as the baseline method,
as it was the best available in terms of material consumption, while still
resulting in support that were printable on FDM 3D printer.
Unfortunately we were unable to compare ourself to Zhang et. al. 2020 and Vaissier et. al. 2019,
as their code was not made publicly available due to some agreement with industrial partners,
and even if we have had access to the code, the resulting supports would have probably be
unprintable, as they were designed for SLS/SLA 3D printers.
The reduction in material consumption that we have achieved is significant;
Even acknowledging that the 43\% of reduction only translate to 10-30 grams 
of material saved per print, the amount of prints in circulation is exorbitant,
and is rapidly increasing, and a few grams saved every print would still result in
a pretty sizable reduction in plastic waste if our system (or a similar one)
was adopted at scale.


Our approach is not without downsides, in particular it require higher computational
costs in the beginning, slightly higher print times, however we think both of this 
factors can be addressed; Indeed the computational costs could be amortized, as most 3D models
are designed once and printed many times, meaning that we could run our algorithm only once, and then
re-use the supports multiple times. The slower printing speed are harder to address, however 
we should note this factor was not considered at all in the cost function, meaning that
some marginal improvements could be achieved simply by correctly codify the additional time.
Overall we think that this thesis has proven the potential that our approach has,
and we believe that it could gain wider adoption once a few key limitations have been addressed.
In the next two sections of this chapter we well take a look at what those limitations are and
how they could be mitigated in future works.

\section{Limitations}

Our work has some limitations and downsides when compared to state of the art support 
structure generation methods. Generally speaking we can divide those limitations 
into two categories: ``structural'' limitations and ``Implementation Specific'' limitations.
The first category of limitations is, as the name imply, structural to the proposed solution
and are tradeoff that will probably remain, regardless of how much effort is put in 
improving our work. On the other hand, the second class of limitation is, as the name imply,
implementation specific, and can therefore improve with potential future work.

\subsection{Structural Limitations}

The topology we have selected for the support structure (namely the rigid frame)
has some structural disadvantage compared to more common topologies such as teh one adopted
by tree supports (\ref{sec:tree_support}).
The two key limitations are:
\begin{description}

\item [Higher printing time] The rigid frame topology require the nozzle to do a lot more travel
in order to deposit the material in the desired places. This of course result in higher print time,
which are not excessive, but are a limitation that is worth considering.

\item [More challenging print] Our support structure are more delicate when compared to the ``Tree support''
or the ``Normal support'' strategies. In our tests we did not exigence substantial challenges in printing
the support structure, however it is possible if not likely that on a not properly tuned 3D printer
our support structure would have a significant higher likelihood of failure compared to the other methods
we mentioned.
    
\end{description}

\subsection{Implementation Specific Limitations}
\label{sec:imp_spec_limit}

Our specific implementation of the support structure optimization algorithm was constructed 
to be a proof of concept, and to answer the specific question of how much material could be saved
with a genetic-based support structure optimizer. For this reason the current solution is not ``Production Ready''
and would require addressing a few limitations before a potential wide adoption. The implementation-specific
limitations that we think should be addressed before any wider adoption are:

\begin{description}
    \item[Contact Points Topology] Our work's primary focus was on the support structure optimization, 
    however the way that contact points are selected, and especially their structure/topology is another important
    part in support structure generation. This part of the algorithm is not as important in the context of material 
    reduction, however it is quite important for the final quality of the printed output.
    As we have seen in section \ref{sec:print_quality}, there is a small but noticeable difference in quality
    between the support generated by our algorithm, and the one generated by the ``Tree Supports''.
    \item[Better Integration with Slicers] Our algorithm simply output a 3D mesh that is then imported
    into the slicer software together with the mesh of the object we want to print. This approach is 
    advantageous in the context of a research project, as it's easy to implement, and is portable across
    multiple slicing software, however in a deployment ``at scale'' it would be ideal to have a more in depth
    integration with a specific slicer, in order to remove the need of multiple passages, that are both
    annoying and time consuming.
    Furthermore a better integration would include some creature comfort feature that are expected nowadays
    on modern slicers, such as the ability to manually add and remove supports or the use of interface layers
    (whish is a techniques that involve printing the last few layer with a lower density, or with a different material
    to ensure the supports are easy to remove).
    \item[Reduciton in parameters numbers and Ease of use] The current algorithm has 60+ tunable parameters
    that are necessary to make the algorithm work. Likely we have found that those parameters generalize well
    (in other words we have used the same parameters for all three of the models we tested); However
    a simplification process where only the more important parameters are exposed, and a good default is found 
    for all the others would be necessary to make this algorithm user-friendly. This task is not necessary
    hard, but it does require a bit of experimentation, ideally on different printers, which make it 
    a bit resource intensive.
\end{description}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

In therms of future work for improving or expanding the capability of our algorithm
we see two broad categories of potential follow ups. We can call the first one 
``Refinement works'' and this include all the few improvements that could 
be made to our algorithm with the aim of making it production ready, and
distributing it to a large user-base; The second one can be called ``Expansions works''
and contains some ideas that were not tested in this thesis, but could provide interesting
new functionalities/capability to our algorithm.

\subsection{Refinement Works}
Refining the work for a wide scale deployment mostly involve fixing
the implementation-specific limitations we listed in section \ref{sec:imp_spec_limit}.
Fixing those limitations would involve: Creating a better algorithm for the generation
of the contact point in order to remove the minor deterioration in quality that we observed when
using our supports; Then we would need to integrate our project into one or more popular slicers,
in order to reduce the friction for using our system; And finally the last step would involve
simplifying the configuration process by finding some good defaults for the parameters
and possibly selecting a subset of important parameters that should be exposed to the user,
while leaving more complex and less important one on a separate ``advanced'' configuration layer.

\subsection{Expansions works}

Our work could be expanded in the future with more features and capability; The cost-function
driven optimization of a genetic algorithm provide quite a bit of flexibility in the optics
of adding new features, as adding some new objective is often as simple as defining a way to measure it
and adding an appropriate component to the cost function.
In this optics there are two key lines of work that we would like to highlight as 
potential future improvement of our work, those are:

\begin{description}
    \item[Costifying Print Time] 
    In the results chapter (\ref{cha:results}) we have noted that our algorithm produces supports that are
    on average take slightly longer to print. This primarily steam from the fact that the ``rigid frame''
    support topology require that the nozzle move around more to deposit the material, however this does
    not mean that this print time can never improve; Indeed our current algorithm is not properly
    costifying the print time, however we can imagine that, as part as a future improvement, a new component
    could be added to the cost function, that properly penalize structures that take too long to print
    therefore reducing this downside.
    \item[Extending to Variable Beam Width] 
    In our current work, the rigid frame we are constructing is made of beams that all have the same
    width. This however is not a fundamental constraint in 3D printing, indeed we could construct
    structures with an arbitrary beam thickness. For this reason we are proposing that, as part of future
    improvements the width of the beam could be an optimized parameter, rather than a simple constant
    defined by the user. This would imply that the algorithm could decide weather it is more advantageous
    to build a support using may smaller beams, or a single larger beam.
    This improvement would go hand in hand with the previous one we listed (the costification of the print time)
    as generally speaking single thinker beams is going to be printed faster compared to a set of 
    smaller beams, meaning that the ability of decide beam width would potentially result 
    in greater time saving when compared to the costification of the print time. 
\end{description}

\subsection*{Closing Remarks}

In conclusion we can affirm that our project fulfilled it's objectives,
as it proven that genetic algorithms could be applied in support structure
optimization for additive manufacturing, and could result in a substantial
saving of materials.
Our project has also shown the key role that stiffness play in this problem
proving that techniques adopted for SLA/SLS 3D printing technologies are not necessary
applicable to FDM printers.
Our final algorithm can be a strong starting point for future improvements that could 
result in smarter support structure optimization algorithm being integrated in modern 
slicers, hopefully resulting in a measurable reduction in plastic consumption.
