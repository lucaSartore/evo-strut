\chapter{Support Optimization Algorithm}
\label{cha:support_opt_alg}


\section{Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:pipeline_overview}

The support structure optimization algorithm we are proposing is organized into a pipeline
composed of 8 steps; of those, the first and last are simple I/O steps (namely Importing and Exporting).
Then the steps two to four (Criticality Detection, Floating Regions Detection and Criticality Grouping)
are simple pre-processing steps that perform some operations in order to prepare data for the following
steps.
Finally steps five to seven (Contact Points Optimization, Contact Points Grouping and Support
Structure Optimization) are the real ``Core'' steps, that perform the optimization
of the actual support. This operation is performed in three stages, rather than a single optimization 
process, for a more efficient computation; in fact we can assume that the decision about where to place
contact points in the areas to support is (roughly) independent from the decision on how to build a
structure that reaches those points, and this allows us to take the decisions separately, and have two simpler
problems instead of one complex one. This approach is quite common in the literature; in fact it has
been used by Zhang et al. \cite{ZHANG2020117} (the second paper we cited in the previous literature chapter).
The following list shows the steps of the pipeline in order, with a small description of what they do:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Importing:} This step loads the model and performs some simple re-meshing operations in order
    to reduce the number of triangles in the mesh in with the objective of obtaining better performance.
    \item \textbf{Criticality Detection:} This step detects the critical regions (the points where the
    surface is too steep, and a support is required).
    \item \textbf{Floating Regions Detection:} This step detects the ``Floating regions'' (areas
    of the model that, while being printed are completely detached from the plate for a period of time,
    as the connection is provided by layers above).
    \item \textbf{Criticality Grouping:} This step groups the critical areas detected in the step 2 into
    some areas that can be optimized separately (for more efficient computation).
    \item \textbf{Contact Points Optimization:} This step optimize a set of points where
    supports will be placed using a genetic algorithm and a cost function.
    \item \textbf{Contact Points Grouping:} This step groups the contact points into areas based on proximity;
    this step is once again performed in order to optimize the different areas separately.
    The optimization is performed using a Compositional Pattern Producing Network Based Genetic Algorithm.
    \item \textbf{Support Structure Optimization:} This step optimizes the support structure using a genetic
    algorithm; Here is where the stiffness evaluation algorithm we developed previously is used.
    \item \textbf{Exporting:} In the final step we export the support structure as a mesh, based 
    on the the shape optimized by the previous sections.
\end{itemize}


\section{Importing}
\label{sec:importing}

The importing stage is conceptually very simple: a mesh of the object we want to generate supports for
is loaded, and then re-meshed.
Re-meshing is a simple procedure that involves starting from a mesh that represents 
a specific object, and then applying a transformation with the aim of obtaining a different
mesh, that represents the same object, using a different set of triangles that satisfy 
some specific properties.
Figure \ref{fig:remeshing} shows three different meshes that represent the same object;
the first one is minimizing the number of triangles necessary to describe the object,
but is resulting in a set of triangles that is very irregular. The second image shows
a re-meshing of the first one, where triangles are divided into smaller and smaller pieces
in order to obtain more regular triangles. The third one is also a re-meshing, but 
it is a more aggressive one, and results in smaller triangles.
For this project we have used an incremental remesher (an algorithm that performs 
re-meshing by means of progressively splitting and merging triangles in order to obtain
some desired properties). The specific implementation was taken from the popular
rust library ``baby-shark'' \cite{babyshark}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/remeshing.png}
    \caption{Example of a re-meshing operation (source: \cite{remeshing})}
    \label{fig:remeshing}
\end{figure}

There are two reasons why re-meshing is applied as part of the loading stage.
The first one is purely a performance reason; in fact in order to generate support structures
we don't need a high definition mesh, instead a good approximation is enough. For this reason
the re-meshing process targets an edge length in the range of 0.3 - 1.0 mm. This provides a sufficient
level of accuracy while also resulting in a manageable number of triangles, even for large meshes,
which helps the algorithm's performance.
The second reason why re-meshing is important has to do with the representation of the mesh
in the algorithm; in fact the last step of the loading procedure is converting 
the mesh into a graph, where every triangle is a node, and each node is connected
to three other neighbors (one for each side of the triangle).
Generally speaking we would want all triangles to be as close to equilateral as possible,
in such a way every connection inside the graph would have roughly the same length; this
fact is a precondition necessary for the correct functioning of the next stages in the pipeline.
The re-meshing algorithm is therefore also used to ensure triangles have roughly the same size,
and are roughly equilateral.

\section{Criticality Detection}
\label{sec:crit_detection}

The first step in the generation of automatic supports is of course knowing what zones must be supported.
Generally speaking the need for a support is usually achieved with a simple check of the 
overhanging angle (as we already saw in the introduction \ref{sec:role_of_sup}).
One of the most basic techniques for criticality detection is to simply take the angle formed by the 
surface normal of a triangle, and a vector facing downward, and compare that to the threshold of what
is considered acceptable, in order to detect the critical areas.
Most 3D printing software uses methods derived from the one we just described with a few
key refinements such as for example bridge detection, detection of areas that are ``floating''
etcetera. Our method is also inspired by the aforementioned basic idea, but is slightly different from the 
ones currently in use in most commercial 3D printing software, as it's built with a different 
paradigm. Instead of simply detecting a simple boolean value that tells if a surface is critical
or not, our method aims to provide a cost function that, if minimized, reduces the amount of critical
areas there are in a specific model. The reason why this approach was chosen is straightforward, as it 
is a simple consequence of the fact that we will need a cost function in order to apply a genetic-algorithm
based optimizer in the following steps.

Now that we explained the reasoning behind the creation of a custom solution for criticality detection
we can begin explaining our implementation. Algorithm \ref{alg:crit_det} shows the pseudo-code of 
our implementation of a criticality detection procedure.
The key idea behind our solution is to assign to every node in the mesh graph a ``criticality''.
Then the critical nodes are the ones that have a criticality different than zero. This model has the benefit
that it can be easily transformed into a cost function by summing all the criticality scores and 
weighting them by the area of the triangle they are associated to.
The second key idea that our solution brings to the table is the concept of ``criticality propagation''.
This concept represents the fact that criticality is not an arbitrary value assigned to every single
triangle, regardless of the neighbors, instead the criticality is ``propagated'' from the bottom to the
top. This means that criticality increases every time the object has an overhanging angle that is too high,
and decreases every time the criticality angle is too low. This is important because otherwise, a single
triangle inside the mesh with a sufficiently low overhanging angle would make an entire zone
less critical, or in the same way a single support could singlehandedly make a large critical area
no longer critical. 

We can get a sense of why propagation is important
by looking at figure \ref{fig:critical_detect_no_prop} and \ref{fig:critical_detect}.
In the former we see what the critical regions are if no propagation is applied, meanwhile in the latter one
we see the same output, but propagation is applied. The most notable difference is in the bottom part
of the dragon's wing, as we can see, in the version with no propagation there is a tiny critical area
in the tip of the wing, but as soon as the overhanging angle is small enough, the critical area disappears.
In contrast in the version that has propagation the criticality starts at a high value in the bottom
part of the wing, and requires a bit of time for the score to reach zero, resulting in a much larger critical
region. This behavior is desirable, as it is unreasonable to think that a single support in the tip of the wing
is enough to support the entire area, meaning that criticality propagation plays a key role in detecting
more realistic critical regions when some parts of the model are ``floating''.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Criticality Detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$ Graph that represents the mesh
    \Ensure $C$ Dictionary that associates a cost with every node in the graph

    \Procedure{LayerOf}{$n$} \Comment{Calculate the layer of node $n$}
        \State \Return $\left\lfloor{\frac{\Call{Height}{n}}{L_{height}}}\right\rfloor$
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex

    \Procedure{PropagationCost}{$G, f, t$} \Comment{Calculate propagation factor of the link $f$(rom) $t$(o)}
        \State $\varphi \gets \Call{AngleBetween}{f, t}$ \Comment{We start from the angle between the two nodes}
        \State $\varphi' \gets \varphi - \varPhi_t$ \Comment{We compare it to the threshold, as small gradients are OK}
        \State $\varphi'' \gets \Call{Clamp}{\varphi', \varPhi_{lb}, \varPhi_{ub}}$ \Comment{We clamp it, to avoid too high a propagation factor}
        \State $d \gets \Call{Distance}{f, t}$ \Comment{Distance factor (long distances are more critical than short ones)}
        \State \Return $K_{prop} \cdot d \cdot \varphi''$
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex

    \Procedure{CriticalityOf}{$G, n, \mathcal{M}$}
        \If {$\Call{Height}{n} = 0$} \Comment{Nodes on the ground are never critical}
            \State \Return 0
        \EndIf
        \State $c \gets $ CRITICALITY\_MAX \Comment{Initialize criticality at max value}
        \For{$a \in \Call{AdjacentNodes}{G, n$}} \Comment{Iterate over adjacent nodes}
            \If {$\Call{LayerOf} {a} <= \Call{LayerOf}{n} \land a \in M$}
                \State $c_a \gets M(a) + \Call{PropagationCost}{a, n}$
                \State $c \gets \Call{Min}{c, c_a}$
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\Call{Max}{c, 0}$ \Comment{Criticality is always greater than or equal to zero}
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex

    \Procedure{PropagateInLayer}{$G, l, \mathcal{M}$}
        \State $ PriorityQueue \gets \emptyset$
        \For{$n$ in $l$} \Comment{Initialize the priority queue with the cost propagated by the layer below}
            \State $c \gets \Call{CriticalityOf}{G, n, \mathcal{M}}$
            \State $\Call{Push}{PriorityQueue, n, c}$
        \EndFor
        \While {$\lnot \Call{Empty}{PriorityQueue}$}
            \State $n, c = \Call{Pop}{PriorityQueue}$ \Comment{Extract node with the lowest criticality}
            \State $\mathcal{M}(n) \gets c$ \Comment{Mark the criticality in the memory}
            \For{$a$ in \Call{Adjacent}{$G, n$}} \Comment{Neighbors might now have lower criticality, update them}
                \State $c \gets \Call{CriticalityOf}{G, a, \mathcal{M}}$
                \State $\Call{TryUpdatePriority}{PriorityQueue, a, c}$
            \EndFor
        \EndWhile
        \State \Return $0$;
    \EndProcedure
    
    \Statex

    \Procedure{CriticalityDetection}{$G$}
        \State $C \gets \emptyset$

        \For{l in \Call{Layers}{$G$}}
            \State \Call{PropagateInLayer}{$G, l, C$} \Comment{Propagate the cost one layer at a time}
        \EndFor

        \State \Return $C$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:crit_det}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Algorithm Overview}
To explain \cref{alg:crit_det} we must start from the concept of layer.
Every node in a graph is assigned to a layer (represented by an integer value)
by the ``LayerOf'' function which is simply placing nodes into ``buckets'' using the node's height.
The reason why we mark the layer is that the criticality can be propagated only from
elements of the same layer, or a layer below; but it can't go ``downward'' (as doing so would imply 
that something that is printed at a certain time is supporting something printed before that time).

The function ``PropagationCost'' takes as input two nodes, and calculates the cost that 
is propagated from one to the other in a specific direction, this means that if
node $A$ has criticality $c_a$ and the propagation cost from node $A$ to node $B$ is
$c_{ab}$ then the cost at node $B$ will be upper bounded by the value $c_a + c_{ab}$.
The reason why we say that is an upper bound is because criticality can always decrease if node $B$ has another neighbor
from which it can propagate a lower cost.
It should be noted that this function can return a negative value, this is the case when 
the angle between the two nodes is below the threshold of the maximum overhanging angle, 
and therefore the criticality should decrease.

The function ``CriticalityOf'' is used to calculate the criticality of a node, starting
from nodes that are close to it; in the function we can see how if the node is close to the
ground the criticality is zero (this is obvious, as if a node is attached to the plate it does
not need any support), otherwise the criticality is initialized to the maximum possible value,
and then is reduced if a path is found to propagate a lower criticality from a neighbor.
In order to be eligible, a neighbor, must be part of a layer that is the same or lower than the node
currently being evaluated.
Now that we have explained the few ``utility'' functions we can tackle the core part of the algorithm,
this is composed of the two functions ``PropagateInLayer'' and ``CriticalityDetection''.
To begin the procedure, nodes in the graph are organized
in layers, and layers are evaluated from the lowest to the highest.
The process of evaluating a layer is essentially one pass of a Dijkstra algorithm, where nodes
are explored from the one with the lowest criticality value to the one with the highest.
This process ensures low time complexity while ensuring that every node has the correct criticality value.
The process is simply repeated for every layer until every node of the graph has a known cost.

\section{Floating Regions Detection}
\label{sec:floating_regions_detection}

Another important pre-processing step is the detection of ``Floating Regions''.
As we mentioned in the introduction, a floating region is defined as a part of the structure that, while being printed,
is not fully connected to the build plate if not for supports. For example
figure \ref{fig:floating_regions} shows the floating regions of the mesh ``Dragon''
highlighted in different colors, while the plain mesh is white.
In this section we will not explain why detecting floating regions is necessary,
as that topic will be extensively covered in one of the next sections (specifically \ref{sec:sup_struct_opt_cf}).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/floating_regions_detect.png}
    \caption{Detected floating regions for the test-mesh ``Dragon''}
    \label{fig:floating_regions}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Algorithm Overview}

Efficient detection of floating regions can be achieved using algorithm \ref{alg:float_det}:
the idea is that the nodes are sorted from the lowest to the highest. Then nodes
are sampled based on the aforementioned  order, and for each of them we execute
an  exploration of the graph, with the node itself being the starting point.
During the exploration, an arch that goes from $A$ to $B$ is 
considered valid only if the layer of $B$ is greater than or equal to the layer of $A$;
this ensures that we cannot go ``downward'' when exploring the graph, and therefore that
we cannot explore a floating region unless we start from a point inside that exact same region.
While the graph is being explored, we mark nodes that have been visited to avoid visiting the same
node twice, and increasing the time complexity. Once an exploration is completed we can decide if
the explored area is a floating region or not. This can be simply done by checking if the height
of the ``source'' node is equal to zero (or at least is below a certain threshold),
if this is the case we can say that the source was attached to the ground, and therefore
the region is not floating. If this is not the case however, the area we have explored must be a 
floating region. We can be sure of this because we are certain that the node we have extracted is the lowest point in the region,
thanks to the sorting we did at the beginning. The sorting also ensures that we start exploring from
the nodes that are close to the ground, avoiding a situation where a floating region is expanded
too much and reaches zones that are actually not considered ``floating''.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Floating Regions Detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$ Graph that represents the mesh
    \Ensure $\mathcal{F}$ Set of floating regions 

    \Procedure{ExploreUpward}{$G, s, V$} \Comment{Collect the component reachable going up from $s$}
        \State $R \gets \emptyset$
        \State $Q \gets \{s\}$ \Comment{Initialize the queue with the node}
        \While{$\lnot \Call{IsEmpty}{Q}$} \Comment{repeat until queue is empty}
            \State $n \gets \Call{Pop}{Q}$
            \State $V(n) \gets true$
            \State $R \gets \Call{Append}{R, n}$
            \For{$a \in \Call{Adjacent}{G, n}$} \Comment{Explore adjacent neighbors}
                \If{ $\Call{LayerOf}{a} \geq \Call{LayerOf}{n} \land \lnot V(a)$ } \Comment{Filter by non visited, and $\geq$ layer}
                    \State \Call{Push}{Q, a}
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndWhile
        \State \Return $R$
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex


    \Procedure{FloatingRegionsDetection}{$G$}
        \State $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset, \quad V \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Detected regions and visited nodes}
        \For{$s \in \Call{SortNodesByHeight}{G}$}
            \If{$V(s)$}
                \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{Already part of a component}
            \EndIf
            \State $R \gets \Call{ExploreUpward}{G, s, V}$ \Comment{Exploring graph, while only moving ``upward''}
            \If{$\Call{HeightOf}{s} > 0$} \Comment{If the node is detached from the ground}
                \State $\mathcal{F} \gets \Call{AddFloatingRegion}{F, R}$ \Comment{Mark the explored node as a floating region}
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\mathcal{F}$
    \EndProcedure
\label{alg:float_det}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\section{Criticality Grouping}
\label{sec:criticality_grouping}

In this stage in the pipeline we have a set of nodes inside the mesh graph that is deemed
``critical'', however we have no grouping or topology of the critical areas themselves.
This is not strictly speaking an issue, as we could in theory execute the next stage (contact
points optimization) only once, for the entire set of critical regions, however this would 
be less than ideal from a performance standpoint. In fact, generally speaking in this kind of 
optimization problem it is more efficient to optimize many small problems rather than
a single larger one. Furthermore due to the way we are defining our cost function (that we will
explain more in section \ref{sec:contact_points_opt_cf}) the cost of one set
of connected critical nodes is independent from the cost of another equivalent set. With those two facts in mind, it is easy to see why grouping the contact points
based on proximity, and optimizing each of them as a separate problem would be convenient
from a performance standpoint, while not impacting the output of the optimizer.
For this reason we made the choice of inserting the ``Criticality Grouping'' stage
in the pipeline, so that we could create a sensible grouping of the critical regions,
with the objective of optimizing them separately.
Algorithm \ref{alg:crit_grouping} shows the procedure of how grouping is executed.

\subsection{Algorithm Overview}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Criticality Grouping}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$ Graph that represents the mesh
    \Require $C$ Set of critical nodes detected
    \Ensure $R$ Grouped set of critical nodes

    \Procedure{Explore}{$G, C, s, V$} \Comment{Collect the critical nodes reachable from $s$}
        \State $R \gets \emptyset$
        \State $Q \gets \{s\}$ \Comment{Initialize the queue with the node}
        \While{$\lnot \Call{IsEmpty}{Q}$} \Comment{repeat until queue is empty}
            \State $n \gets \Call{Pop}{Q}$
            \State $V(n) \gets true$
            \State $R \gets \Call{Append}{R, n}$
            \For{$a \in \Call{Adjacent}{G, n}$} \Comment{Explore adjacent neighbors}
                \If{ $n \in C \land \lnot V(a)$ } \Comment{Filter by non visited, and critical nodes}
                    \State \Call{Push}{Q, a}
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndWhile
        \State \Return $R$
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex


    \Procedure{CriticalityGrouping}{$G, C$}
        \State ${R} \gets \emptyset, \quad V \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Grouped regions and visited nodes}
        \State ${N} \gets \Call{Dilation}{G, start=C, max\_distance=D_{max}}$
        \State $C \gets C \cup N$ \Comment{Consider critical all nodes that are closer than $D_{max}$ to a critical node}
        \For{$s \in C$}
            \If{$V(s)$}
                \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{Already part of a component}
            \EndIf
            \State $C \gets \Call{Explore}{G, C, s, V}$ \Comment{Exploring graph, while only moving ``upward''}
            \State $R \gets \Call{AddGroup}{R, C}$
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $R$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:crit_grouping}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent
In the algorithm (\ref{alg:crit_grouping}) we can see that the ``grouping'' operation mainly
involves two steps:
\begin{description}
    \item[Dilation Step:] 
    In this step we apply a ``dilation'' to the critical nodes. This dilation
    is characterized by a specific parameter ($D_{max}$) that represents the distance of a dilation.
    During the dilation operation, every node that has at least one critical node closer than $D_{max}$
    is considered critical as well. This dilation is applied in order to ``merge'' critical regions
    that are really close together with the objective or reducing the total number of regions the algorithm 
    will produce. This might seem counter intuitive, as we previously claimed that many smaller problems
    are easier to solve then a larger one, however this general rule has a limit,
    and we found that, if a mesh has a large area that is very close to the overhanging angle, the
    criticality detection algorithm would generate a very large subset of tiny critical areas, which is undesirable.
    For this reason the dilation was added so that by tuning $D_{max}$
    we are able to control how ``aggressively'' critical regions are divided into groups.
    If $D_{max}$ is set to zero we get the highest number of groups, while if we increase the value
    we gradually reduce the amount of groups that are generated.
    \item[Grouping Step:] 
    The grouping step is then achieved with a simple exploration of the graph itself,
    in this stage we start from every critical point, and we explore the graph by navigating
    to all the nodes that are marked as ``critical'' (but not traversing any non-critical node), 
    in this stage we keep track of the nodes that are 
    visited so as to avoid visiting the same node twice, and increasing the time complexity.
    And finally, once all the nodes that are reachable from a source have been found, we can mark those nodes
    as a ``critical group'' (i.e. a set of critical nodes that are all connected to each other).
\end{description}



\section{Contact Points Optimization}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt}

The objective of this stage is to decide the ``contact points'' (i.e. where the supports
will make contact with the printed object in order to provide a support interface).
This step is done independently from the true optimization of the support structure.
Generally speaking it is not true that the two choices are independent from each other; in fact
we can easily imagine a scenario where placing a contact point in a specific position
reduces the cost function for the contact points by a bit, but introduces more cost
in the generation of the support structure. However, even though they are not purely independent
from each other, they are not excessively correlated, meaning that separating the two stages
does not introduce excessive quality loss. For this reason separating the two stages
can be considered a good idea; in fact other related works such as Zhang et al. 2020 \cite{ZHANG2020117}
have done exactly the same. Our method is not drastically different from theirs,
it of course has a slightly different cost function and optimization process, but 
realistically speaking, in terms of novelty our approach for contact points optimization
does not have much to offer (the same cannot be said for the next two optimization stages).
The optimization of contact points is achieved with a genetic algorithm, therefore
the inner working of the algorithm is explained in separate sections,
one for every component of the genetic algorithm (cost function, gene, mutation, crossover
and optimization process).
Figure \ref{fig:contact_points_opt} shows the result of the optimization process, where the contact
points are represented with a small purple dot on top of the critical area (the radius however is
not visualized). We can see how the result is not particularly interesting, as it basically looks like
a sample of equally spaced points inside the critical area. This makes sense as generally speaking
a good support structure satisfies exactly that condition, however this shows us how complex
genetic-algorithm optimization is not always necessary for every task. In fact this optimization
stage is probably the least useful of the three, and could easily be replaced by a simpler and faster
``greedy'' algorithm that would perform very similarly.

\subsection{Genome Structure}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_gs}

The genetic representation of a contact points choice is incredibly straightforward:
it is essentially a set of the nodes in the graph that we decided to place a support on,
with every node having a ``radius'' associated with it (allowing the algorithm
a bit of flexibility in deciding whether it is more advantageous to use one large
support or many smaller supports).
From this gene it is possible to derive the set of supported nodes;
this is done by drawing a circle around the node, and selecting the supports
that are on the perimeter (the use of the perimeter rather than all the circle's area
is due to the way supports are generated. Since they are ``empty'' cones, they
do not provide any support in the center of the cone's base, but only on the perimeter;
nevertheless this part will be explained more thoroughly in the exporting section \ref{sec:exporting}).

\subsection{Cost Function}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_cf}

The cost function is derived from the criticality detection algorithm \cref{alg:crit_det}.
As we explained in section \ref{sec:crit_detection} the output of the algorithm is
a dictionary that maps every node in the surface graph to its criticality value, and
starting from this dictionary it is possible to find the total cost by summing
all the individual costs and weighting them by the node's area.
Nonetheless we are still required to make a few changes to the algorithm
in order to use it as the cost function.
The necessary changes can be seen in algorithm \ref{alg:contact_points_opt_cf},
the first one (at line 2) is straightforward, as it simply 
introduces in the evaluation procedure the effect of supports. In fact the previous version
of the algorithm did not consider supports at all, now however
we must keep track of where the supports are, and in the case that node $n$
is one of the nodes supported we automatically assign its criticality to zero.
The second change to the algorithm is a bit less trivial to explain.
We can see that at line 6 we added to the final cost a portion of the ``base cost''.
The base cost of $n$ is defined as the cost that node $n$ would have, if no supports were applied.
$\alpha$ is a small scalar (in our final tests we used 0.03) that represents how much of the
base cost is ``passed'' back when evaluating the structure with supports enabled.
The reason for this modeling choice has to do with floating regions.
As we can remember from section \ref{sec:crit_detection} the propagation factor
was added to avoid detecting only a tiny critical region in some particular
scenarios such as for example the wing of the dragon 
(see figure \ref{fig:critical_detect} and \ref{fig:critical_detect_no_prop}).
Without this cost term, a single well-placed support in the ``tip'' of the dragon's wing
would singlehandedly remove the entire critical region, however in real life such a
support would create many problems, both because of the z-wobble effect (\ref{sec:wobble-test})
and because when printing the connection between supports and the model is constructed
to be fragile on purpose, in order to simplify the removal process, making it extremely
likely that the wing would detach from the support before it manages to connect to the body
of the dragon.
The addition of the base cost makes it so that, when propagating,
the criticality value has a tendency to drift towards the value it would have
had in the case where no supports were added. This is a simple trick that allows
floating regions to be handled correctly and avoids the issues we described.


\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Updates to criticality }
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Procedure{CriticalityOf}{$G, n, \mathcal{M}$}
        \If {$\Call{Height}{n} = 0 \lor \textcolor{red}{\Call{IsSupported}{n}}$}
            \State \Return 0
        \EndIf
        \State ...
        \State \Return $\Call{Max}{c, 0} + \textcolor{red}{\alpha \cdot \Call{BaseCost}{n}}$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:contact_points_opt_cf}
\end{algorithm}

The cost associated with the criticality is only half of the equation of course;
in fact if that was the only cost we applied to the optimizer the optimal solution
would be as simple as placing a support on every critical node in the graph.
This is of course ideal from a quality of support standpoint, but is less than ideal
if we consider the cost of the material used for the supports.
For this reason the second part of the cost is simply derived from a unit cost
for every support placed, plus a linear cost proportional to the area of the support
circle.

\subsection{Mutations}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_mut}

Since the gene we used to represent the contact points is quite simple, the
gene mutations applied can also be simple. We have designed four fundamental
mutations, so that every time a gene needs to be mutated we can simply
select one or more fundamental mutations, and apply them sequentially to the gene.
The four fundamental mutations are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Add Support:} This mutation picks a random node from the list of critical
    ones and places a new support with a randomly generated radius on it.
    \item \textbf{Remove Support:} This mutation picks a random support from the gene
    and removes it.
    \item \textbf{Move Support:} This mutation picks a random support, and moves it to a different
    node, when selecting the new node only nodes that are within a certain radius of the original
    one are considered eligible.
    \item \textbf{Change Support Radius:} This mutation increases or decreases the radius
    of the support by some random value, while ensuring the radius stays within some
    predetermined margins.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crossover}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_cross}

The crossover strategy is simple to explain, given two genes, a random plane is generated
in 3D space, then we can create a new gene by selecting all the points that are on one side
of the plane from Parent A, and all the points that are on the other side of the plane from 
Parent B.
Figure \ref{fig:crossover_cpo} shows this process in action with a two dimensional representation
of a set of points.
The generation of the random plane is done by selecting three random points from one parent 
($P_A$, $P_B$, and $P_{\perp}$) and then selecting the plane $\pi$ such that the first two points
are included in the plane, and such that the plane is perpendicular to the plane $\pi*$ that includes
all three points.
Algorithm \cref{alg:plane_construction} shows a simple procedure to construct such a plane.
The reason this plane is constructed in this specific manner (rather than simply constructing
a plane that includes three random points) is that support structures tend to be mostly flat,
therefore with a plane that includes three points it is likely that most points would end up
close to the border, making the division not very equal.
In the algorithm described there is one small edge case; in fact it cannot work if there are
less than three points in a plane, or if the three points selected are in a straight line, 
making it impossible to build the plane. In all of these circumstances the algorithm simply
ignores the crossover and returns a gene identical to one random parent.


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/crossover_cpo.png}
    \caption{Example of how the crossover is made (in 2 dimensions)}
    \label{fig:crossover_cpo}
\end{figure}


\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Plane Construction}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $p_A, p_B$ Points defining the base line of the plane
    \Require $p_{\perp}$ Point from which distance is maximized
    \Ensure $\pi$ The desired plane

    \Procedure{ConstructPlane}{$p_A, p_B, p_{\perp}$} \Comment{Find the plane that includes the three points}
        \State $\pi^* \gets \Call{PlaneFromPoints}{p_A, p_B, p_{\perp}}$
        \State $\mathbf{v} \gets \Call{Normal}{\pi^*}$ \Comment{Normal of plane spanned by $p_A, p_B, p_{\perp}$}
        \State $p_C \gets p_A + \mathbf{v}$ \Comment{Construct third point along the normal direction}
        \State $\pi \gets \Call{PlaneFromPoints}{p_A, p_B, p_C}$ \Comment{Find the plane that includes the three points}
        \State \Return $\pi$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:plane_construction}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Initialization}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_init}

The initialization of the genes is done with a simple procedure: first we calculate the area
of the critical surface we need to generate, then we calculate the number of supports that 
need to be generated initially by multiplying the area by a ``support density'' parameter,
finally we randomly generate the desired number of supports by repeatedly randomly
sampling a node, randomly selecting a radius, and adding a support on the selected node
with the selected radius.


\subsection{Optimization Process and Parameters}
\label{sec:contact_points_opt_par}

The optimization process is fairly straightforward, it is composed of at most 2000 generations,
each of them having 100 individuals. Patience is used as an early termination strategy to avoid wasting
CPU cycles, and the maximum number of generations allowed without progress is set to 25.
The selection process is elitist, with the 10 best individuals always passing to the next generation,
while the selection of the remaining 90 individuals is done using tournament selection with a 
tournament size of 10.



\section{Contact Points Grouping}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping}

This stage in the pipeline aims to group contact points based on proximity, in order to optimize
each section individually. As in the previous ``grouping'' step, this is not strictly necessary
theoretically, however it is essentially required for good performance in practice.
The reason why splitting is acceptable in this scenario is that the support structures
of areas that are far apart are generally independent from each other. For example,
in our ``Dragon'' testcase we can imagine how the structure generated to support the left
wing is roughly independent from the structure generated for the right wing.
For this reason optimizing them together gives minimal benefit to the objective function,
however optimizing them separately has big performance benefits. This is because the algorithm
for stiffness evaluation we explained in chapter \ref{cha:stiffness_algo} has a complexity
of $o(n^2 \log (n))$ meaning that a divide and conquer approach can be highly effective
in reducing the computational complexity of the algorithm.
Initially some simpler ``greedy'' algorithms for the grouping of contact points were tested,
however none of them provided satisfactory results. These tests included strategies such as using the
``critical areas'' as a starting point, and merging them based on proximity, and other similar
heuristics. However we found that with those types of approach it was extremely difficult to regulate
the right amount of ``grouping'' required for different shapes, as they tended to either
group too aggressively, or not group enough. For this reason an optimization-based
solution was adopted using a Compositional Pattern Producing Network for generating the groups,
with a genetic algorithm based optimization.
The final result of a grouping step executed on the ``Dragon'' testcase can be seen in figure
\ref{fig:cp_grouping}. To explain the optimization process that led to the result seen in the
picture we will explain each component of the genetic algorithm
individually as we did in the previous section.

    \caption{An example of how contact points are grouped}

\subsection{Genome Structure}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_gs}

In the contact point grouping stage the ``gene'' is represented as a Compositional Pattern
Producing Network (CPPN) that takes as input the position of a contact point (the $x$, $y$ and $z$ coordinates)
and gives as output the group that the node is part of.
To categorize a point into a group we just take the last layer of the network, and pick the group
corresponding to the neuron with the highest output. This means that the maximum number of groups
the network can produce is upper bounded by the size of the network. Generally speaking this is not an
issue as the number of groups generated is not particularly high, meaning that even with a small
25 neurons output layer we are not placing significant constraints on the model.
The final topology of the network ended up as a fully connected NN, with the input layer
of size 3, one hidden layer with size of 32, and a Sigmoid activation function, and one 
output layer with a size of 25.
This neural network is tiny by modern standards, and this is done for two reasons: first of all
the task itself is not very complicated, and can be easily performed with such a small network, so
there is no real reason for going bigger. Secondly the training process is not really efficient as
it relies on genetic evolution rather than back-propagation, making shallower networks ideal since they
are easier to train.

\subsection{Cost Function}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_cf}

The cost function associated with grouping is parametrized by three values
($K_{\text{height}}$, $K_{\text{area}}$ and $K_{\text{volume}}$).
Their use is illustrated in algorithm \ref{alg:cpg_cf}.
The general idea is that once a group is created we compute its (three dimensional) convex hull
(using the efficient Quick Hull algorithm \cite{quickhull}),
and then apply three main costs.
The first two are proportional to the area and the volume of the convex hull, and are
essentially there to incentivize the algorithm to create groups that contain points
that are close together, in order to minimize the cost. Meanwhile the third one is
a cost that is proportional to the height of the group. This is a regularization term
that ensures there is a cost in creating a group, otherwise the optimizer would always end up
with a solution that uses the highest number of groups possible.
Generally speaking it is less desirable to create too many groups, because we give less
opportunities to the optimizer to ``merge'' support structures together, and therefore
save on material; This ``undesirability'' is higher for groups with a taller height (defined
as the maximum $z$ of the points in the group), because the taller a point is, the more expensive
it becomes to generate a support structure that reaches it; This is why the regularization factor
depends on the height, and is not just a constant.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Contact Points Grouping Cost Function}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$ Set of grouped contact points
    \Ensure $c$ The total cost

    \Procedure{ConstructPlane}{$G$} \Comment{Compute the cost of a set of groups}
        \State $c \gets 0$
        \For {$g \in G$}
            \State $h \gets \Call{ConvexHull}{g}$
            \State $c \gets c + 
                K_{\text{height}} \cdot \Call{Height}{h} +
                K_{\text{area}} \cdot \Call{Area}{h} +
                K_{\text{volume}} \cdot \Call{Volume}{h}$
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $c$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:cpg_cf}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Mutations}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_mut}

Defining mutations on a neural network (especially if the network is small and shallow like ours)
is particularly easy, as the network can be seen as a set of weights we need to tune. For this reason
the mutation is parametrized by 4 parameters: a mutation rate ($p_m$), a reset rate ($p_r$)
and two random distributions for mutation and reset ($\mathcal{D}_m$ and $\mathcal{D}_r$).
With these four parameters the mutation can be easily described with algorithm \ref{alg:network_mut}.
In the final implementation we ended up defining four different sets of the four parameters,
and randomly picking one for every mutation operation.
This was done in order to increase the stochasticity of the mutation, that is generally
speaking a good idea for genetic algorithms.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Mutation of neural network}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $N$: The network to mutate
    \Require $p_m \in [0, 1]$: Neuron mutation probability
    \Require $p_r \in [0, 1 - p_m]$: Neuron reset probability
    \Require $\mathcal{D}_m$: Arbitrary probability distribution for mutation 
    \Require $\mathcal{D}_r$: Arbitrary probability distribution for reset
    \Ensure $N^*$: The mutated network

    \Procedure{MutateNetwork}{$G$} \Comment{Compute the cost of a set of groups}
        \State $N^* \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Initialize empty network}
        \For {$n \in G$} \Comment{Iterate over the parameters of $G$}
            \State Sample $x \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$
            \If{$x < p_m$} \Comment{Weight mutation}
                \State Sample $m \sim \mathcal{D}_m$
                \State $n^* \gets n + m$
            \ElsIf{$x < p_m + p_r$} \Comment{Weight reset}
                \State Sample $r \sim \mathcal{D}_r$
                \State $n^* \gets r$
            \Else
                \State $n^* \gets n$
            \EndIf
            \State $N^* \gets \Call{AddParameter}{G^*, n^*}$ \Comment {Adding the mutated weight to the new network}
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $N^*$ \Comment {Returning the fully mutated network}
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:network_mut}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Crossover}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_cross}

Crossover is once again simple in the case of neural networks (especially
considering that the size and shape of the network is known ``a priori'' and is
not part of the crossover/mutation), in this case we have defined three different crossover
strategies, and every time a crossover is executed we randomly pick one of them.
The three strategies selected are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform:} This is the traditional uniform crossover where every node in the offspring's network
    is either inherited from Parent A or from Parent B with 50-50 probability.
    \item \textbf{Single Point:} In this case a random number is generated between zero and the number of parameters,
    and then all of the weights that have an index lower than the generated number are inherited from one
    parent, while the remaining ones are inherited from the other.
    \item \textbf{Arithmetic:} In this case we decide a parameter $\alpha$ (usually 0.5) and then the weights of the
    offspring ($\mathbf{\theta}_{o}$) can be expressed as a weighted summation of the weights of the two parents
    ($\mathbf{\theta}_{A}$ and $\mathbf{\theta}_{B}$) using the simple equation: $\mathbf{\theta}_{o} = \alpha \mathbf{\theta}_{A} + (1 - \alpha)\mathbf{\theta}_{B}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Initialization}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_init}

Initialization of the network is achieved using the famous ``He'' initialization technique
first described in the famous 2015 paper ``Delving Deep into Rectifiers: Surpassing Human-Level Performance on ImageNet Classification''
\cite{he2015}.
``Xavier'' initialization \cite{xavier2010} was also tested, but was found to have slightly worse
performance compared to He.




\subsection{Optimization Process and Parameters}
\label{sec:contact_points_grouping_par}

The optimization process is composed of at most 2000 generations,
each of them having 100 individuals. Patience is used as an early termination strategy to avoid wasting
CPU cycles, and the maximum number of generations allowed without progress is set to 100.
The selection process is elitist, with the 10 best individuals always passing to the next generation,
while the selection of the remaining 90 individuals is done using tournament selection with a 
tournament size of 10.
During our testing we found that this optimization problem was really sensitive to the initialization
of the neural network. Therefore, to reduce variability the optimization process is repeated 5 times,
and only the best output is selected. The decision of doing so was also made easier by the fact 
that this step is by far the fastest, taking no more than a few seconds.

\section{Support Structure Optimization}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt}

The support structure optimization is the final major step in the pipeline.
This involves creating a structure that manages to reach all the decided contact points,
while minimizing a cost function that ensures the minimization of the material consumed,
and maximizes the printability by applying some cost function over stiffness and overhanging
angles.
As we explained more thoroughly in the motivation chapter (\ref{cha:motivations})
this stage is arguably the most novel aspect of our work, primarily
due to the fact that none of the works we have mentioned in the previous literature
contain the notion of ``Support Stiffness''.
The optimization algorithm can be seen at work in this
YouTube video: \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=muGUorMoAzM},
or in figure \ref{fig:struct_opt} where an extrapolation of six 
frames is displayed.
For explaining this optimizer we will follow the same approach
as the other two, meaning that we will explain the individual components
of the genetic algorithm starting as always with the gene structure.

\subsection{Genome Structure}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_gs}

The genome for the support structure optimization can be described as a set of $m$ individual
genes:
$$\mathbf{G} = \{ g_1, g_2, ..., g_m\}$$
Each gene $g_i$ is represented by two parameters: a position in 3D space ($p_i$) and a number
$n$ (that varies from 1 to 3) that defines the number of neighbors that the point connects to. In mathematical terms:
$$g_i = (p_i, n_i) \qquad \forall i \in [1, m]$$
This representation is quite simple indeed, however the slightly more complicated part
is transforming this simple encoding into a rigid frame. This is done with algorithm
\ref{alg:gene_to_frame}.
As we can see in the algorithm, every gene $g_i$ is converted into a node in the rigid
frame $F$. Then every node is connected to at most $n_i$ neighbors below that provide
support. Neighbors are eligible if the connection angle is not too steep in order to
ensure that the frames produced are printable by guaranteeing at construction time
that the frame will not have excessive overhanging angles.
Finally there are two minor details that are worth mentioning.
The first one is that all nodes that don't have any support are automatically 
connected to the ground; this is of course necessary if we want the structure to not float in the void.
The second one is that the connections to the contact points (i.e. the points of contact
between the support frame and the printed object) are treated almost in the same way as
regular nodes with the only two differences being that the number of neighbors ($n$)
is automatically set as one, and that those points are not eligible for being ``supporters''
(this is to ensure that a contact point is not connected to another contact point with a beam
in the final structure, which of course would be less than desirable).
Figure \ref{fig:sup_struct_opt_gene_struct} shows the process of converting a gene
(shown in the picture on the left) into a full rigid frame (shown in the picture 
to the right).



\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Construction of a rigid frame starting from a gene}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$: The support structure genome (genes $g_i = (p_i, n_i)$)
    \Require $C$: The contact points with the structure
    \Ensure $F$: The rigid frame

    \Procedure{FindSupporters}{$S, p, n$} \Comment{Up to $n$ nodes below $p$ that can hold it}
        \State $E \gets \{ q \in S : \Call{OverhangingAngle}{q, p} > \varPhi_{max} \}$ \Comment{Eligible if the beam is not too steep}
        \If{$E \neq \emptyset$}
            \State \Return $\Call{Nearest}{E, p, n}$ \Comment{The $n$ closest eligible nodes}
        \EndIf
        \State \Return $\{ (p_x, p_y, 0) \}$ \Comment{None found: drop a beam straight to the ground}
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex

    \Procedure{GeneToFrame}{$G, C$}
        \State $F \gets \emptyset, \quad S \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Frame nodes and candidate supporters}
        \State $Q \gets G \cup \{ (p, 1) : p \in C \}$ \Comment{Contacts are fixed genes with a single neighbor}
        \For{$(p, n) \in \Call{SortByHeightAscending}{Q}$} \Comment{Process from the lowest node to the highest}
            \State $\Call{AddNode}{F, p}$
            \For{$s \in \Call{FindSupporters}{S, p, n}$}
                \State $\Call{AddLink}{F, p, s}$ \Comment{Connect $p$ to its supporting neighbor below}
            \EndFor
            \If{$(p, n) \in G$}
                \State $S \gets S \cup \{ p \}$ \Comment{Only genes (not contacts) can support other nodes}
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $F$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:gene_to_frame}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/sup_struct_opt_gene_struct.png}
    \caption{Genome structure for the support structure optimization step}
    \label{fig:sup_struct_opt_gene_struct}
\end{figure}

Generally speaking the primary challenge when designing the genetic representation
of any genetic-based solver is striking a balance between a complex formulation 
that is able to be very expressive, and a simpler formulation that is easy to optimize
using crossover and mutation.
The genetic representation we presented is a good middle ground between the two 
constraints. The fact that we only allow each node to connect to the $n$ closest eligible
neighbors is of course a reduction in the expressiveness of the system, however
this downside comes with the benefit of a much simpler representation that is easier to optimize.
During the design process more options were considered and tested.
Some of the options that were tested include:
a representation that organized the support structure into layers, where each layer was 
able to be mutated separately, and then the crossover was achieved by merging different layers.
This representation was easy to optimize, however the topology of the output frame was found 
to be too restricted, and the results were not satisfactory.
Another representation that was tested involved using a Compositional Pattern Producing Network
that essentially had as output a multi dimensional vector field, that was then used to generate
the rigid frame. This version however although quite interesting in theory failed to deliver
acceptable results in practice; the primary issue stemmed from the relation
between the size of the network and the ability to train one efficiently.
Small networks were able to be trained effectively using this method, however the resulting 
frames were not good as the network was too small to learn any meaningful pattern.
Increasing the size of the network would have resolved this issue, however the fact that we are
optimizing the network using a genetic algorithm (rather than a gradient-based method)
makes the optimization process much slower and more inefficient, effectively limiting the
size of the networks we can train in a reasonable amount of time.


\subsection{Cost Function}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_cf}

The cost function is composed of four different components.
The four components are the ``Stiffness Cost'' ($C_\text{stiff}$) 
the ``Steepness Cost'' ($C_\text{steep}$), the ``Material Cost'' ($C_\text{mat}$)
and the ``Collision Cost'' ($C_\text{coll}$).
Each of them has a specific objective ranging from the reduction 
of material used to the printability of the structures. The final cost ($C_\text{tot}$)
is obtained with a simple summation of the four components
$$
C_\text{tot} = C_\text{stiff} + C_\text{steep} + C_\text{mat} + C_\text{coll}
$$
This implies that we made the choice of merging the different objectives into one,
and thus optimizing a simple single-objective optimization problem.
This is generally a faster and easier problem to optimize compared to 
multi-objective optimization problems.
Furthermore there are not many tangible benefits in trying to find a Pareto
front of solutions when at the end only one support structure will be used for the actual print.

\subsubsection{Stiffness Cost}

We already discussed extensively the reason why it is essential to ensure that the support
remains stiff while it is being printed. To summarize it in one sentence we can say that ensuring
the support has a sufficient level of stiffness throughout the print process is crucial for
a successful print, failing to provide the required stiffness would result in an inadequate
deposition of the filament due to the movement caused 
by the z-wobble effect \ref{sec:wobble-test}.
Furthermore we have discussed in depth how we can define a stiffness/flexibility matrix,
and how we can calculate them in chapter \ref{cha:stiffness_algo}.
This section aims to put everything together and provide the final formulation of the stiffness
cost function. Algorithm \ref{alg:stiffness_cost} shows the full procedure necessary
for cost calculation. We can see in the function ``StiffnessCost'' that the frame is gradually
constructed one node at a time; this ensures we can calculate the stiffness of every point
while it's being built, and not just with the final structure, that as we discussed in
section \ref{sec:stiffness_part_frames} is quite important for a few mechanical reasons.
While the frame is being constructed two different sources of costs are evaluated. The first one
is simply a function of the stiffness of each beam, this value is calculated
inside the function ``BeamCost'' and is essentially a linear with threshold function of the compliance,
sampled in a set of equally spaced points inside the beam.
The compliance is calculated inside the ``Compliance'' function, and we can see that it is a simple
L2 norm of the $xx$ and $yy$ components of the flexibility matrix $\mathbf{D}$.
The $xx$($yy$) component of $\mathbf{D}$ is essentially expressing what is the displacement
caused along the $x$($y$) axis, assuming that a force is applied parallel to the $x$($y$) axis.
The second cost that is considered is the ``floating regions'' cost. The definition and detection
of floating regions is covered in section \ref{sec:floating_regions_detection}, however we haven't
explained yet why floating regions are important. In a traditional ``critical area'' (i.e. an area
where the overhanging angle is above a specific threshold) the support is placed to sustain the melted
filament while it cools down, but once the cooling process has concluded the support is no longer necessary.
In the case of floating regions however the printed object is (at first) not directly connected to the plate
if not for the supports themselves, so the usefulness of the support is not limited to the first few seconds 
after the filament is being extruded, but it remains crucial until the floating regions are connected
to the plate by means other than the support structure. This means that, in the period of time when the floating region is not connected
to the build plate, the support must provide an adequate level of stiffness to the floating region,
to avoid having to deal with the z-wobble effect (\ref{sec:wobble-test}).
For this reason the function ``FloatingRegionCost'' calculates the equivalent stiffness (that is approximated by calculating the stiffness of a point
in the center of the floating regions connected to all contact points) of every floating region.
This equivalent stiffness is then simply converted to compliance and transformed into cost with a simple
linear with threshold model.
The threshold value and weight are parameters of the floating region itself (with larger floating regions
requiring more stiffness).
Disregarding the application of this cost would result in a support structure such as the one shown in figure
\ref{fig:no_floating_regions_cost}, where we can see that the entire floating region is being supported by a
single beam. This would be fine if we only needed support while the filament is cooling, however
since while the print is ongoing the floating region is connected to the plate only 
by the single support beam the z-wobble effect would cause serious quality issues.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth]{images/no_floating_regions_cost.png}
    \caption{Optimization result if no floating region cost is applied}
    \label{fig:no_floating_regions_cost}
\end{figure}


% \begin{breakablealgorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Support Structure Stiffness Cost}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
% \algnobreaks   
    \Require $F$ The rigid frame, with nodes sorted by height
    \Require $\mathcal{R}$ The set of floating regions
    \Ensure $c$ The total stiffness cost

    \Procedure{Compliance}{$\mathbf{K}$} \Comment{Calculate the compliance value of a stiffness matrix}
        \State $\mathbf{D} \gets \mathbf{K}^{-1}$ \Comment{Calculate the flexibility matrix}
        \State \Return $\sqrt{\mathbf{D}_{xx}{}^2 + \mathbf{D}_{yy}{}^2}$ \Comment{L2 norm of the flexibility along $x$ and $y$}
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex
    % \algmaybreak

    \Procedure{BeamCost}{$F, s, n$} \Comment{Penalize excessive compliance along the beam $s \to n$}
        \State $\mathbf{K}_s \gets \Call{StiffnessOf}{F, s}$
        \State $c \gets 0$
        \For{$p \in \Call{Sample}{s, n, \delta}$} \Comment{Sample points moving from $s$ towards $n$ with a spacing of $\delta$}
            \State $\mathbf{K}_p \gets \Call{Series}{\mathbf{K}, s, p}$ \Comment{Stiffness at the sampled point $p$}
            \State $\gamma \gets \Call{Compliance}{\mathbf{K}_p}$
            \State $c \gets c + \lambda \cdot \Call{Max}{\gamma - \gamma_{th}, 0}$ \Comment{Cost only for compliance above the threshold}
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $c$
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex
    % \algmaybreak

    \Procedure{FloatingRegionCost}{$F, r$} \Comment{Penalize floating regions that are not stiff enough}
        \State $p \gets \Call{CenterOf}{r}$
        \State $F \gets \Call{AddNode}{F, p}$ \Comment{Add a node $p$ at the center of the region $r$}
        \For{$p' \in r$}
            \State $F \gets \Call{AddBeam}{F, p, p'}$ \Comment{Connect $p$ to all contact points in the floating region}
        \EndFor
        \State $\mathbf{K}_p \gets \Call{StiffnessOf}{F, p}$ \Comment{Evaluate stiffness in $p$}
        \State $\gamma \gets \Call{Compliance}{\mathbf{K}_p}$
        \State \Return $r_{\text{weight}} \cdot \Call{Max}{ \gamma - r_{\gamma\text{th}}, 0}$ \Comment{Apply cost based on the compliance in $p$}
    \EndProcedure

    \Statex
    % \algmaybreak

    \Procedure{StiffnessCost}{$F, \mathcal{R}$}
        \State $c \gets 0$
        \State $F' \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Initialize an empty frame}
        \For{$n \in F$} \Comment{Iterate over the elements of the frame (sorted by height)}
            \State $F' \gets \Call{AddNodeAndBeams}{F', n}$ \Comment{Gradually construct $F'$ one node at a time}
            \For{$s \in \Call{Supporters}{F', n}$} \Comment{Apply a cost for every beam supporting $n$}
                \State $c \gets c + \Call{BeamCost}{F', s, n}$ 
                \For {$r \in \mathcal{R}\quad \text{t.c.}\quad r \subset F'$} \Comment{Iterate over floating regions fully included in $F'$}
                    \State $c \gets c + \Call{FloatingRegionCost}{F', r}$ \Comment{Apply the relative cost}
                    \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \mathcal{R} \setminus \{r\}$ \Comment{Remove $r$ from $\mathcal{R}$ to avoid applying cost twice}
                \EndFor
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $c$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:stiffness_cost}
% \end{breakablealgorithm}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Steepness Cost}

Another undesirable feature of the generated supports is them having
an overhanging angle (or steepness) that is too low. If this was the case
the support structure would itself not be printable without
some kind of support, resulting in a problem with infinite recursion.
For this reason a simple cost based on the steepness of the
beams is applied in order to encourage the optimizer to produce structures
that are fully printable.
It should be said that due to the way we convert the genome into a rigid
frame, we could ensure that all overhanging angles are above a certain threshold
by setting $\varPhi_{{max}}$ (line 2 of algorithm \ref{alg:gene_to_frame})
to an appropriate value, however the ability to apply a separate cost function
to the steepness of support is still a desirable trait, as it allows
expressing more complex requirements such as for example: ``You must keep all overhanging
angles above $30^\circ$, and preferably above $45^\circ$, with a certain penalty
applied for every beam with an overhanging angle in the $35^\circ$ to
$45^\circ$ range''.
Calculating the steepness cost is incredibly straightforward,
and the full procedure is shown in algorithm \ref{alg:steepness_cost}.
The algorithm iterates over all the beams of the frame $F$ and applies a cost 
based on the overhanging angle, and on the length of the beam (with
longer beams being weighted more in the cost function).

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Support Structure Steepness Cost}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $F$ The rigid frame
    \Ensure $c$ The total steepness cost

    \Procedure{SteepnessCost}{$F$}
        \State $c \gets 0$
        \For{$(a, b) \in \Call{Beams}{F}$} \Comment{Iterate over every beam once}
            \State $\varphi \gets \Call{HorizonAngle}{a, b}$ \Comment{Inclination of the beam w.r.t. the horizon}
            \State $d \gets \Call{Distance}{a, b}$
            \State $c \gets c + \Call{Max}{\varPhi_{th} - \varphi, 0} \cdot d$ \Comment{Penalize beams less steep than the threshold}
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $K_{steep} \cdot c$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:steepness_cost}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Material Cost}

If we want to minimize the use of material we of course must apply a cost that tells the optimizer
to do so. In the previous literature we have looked at in chapter \ref{cha:prev_literature}
this cost was a function of the volume of the supports, however this mostly stemmed from the fact
that their focus was primarily SLS 3D printing, where printing empty objects is not possible (as the
powder will remain trapped inside). Our work however is more focused on FDM 3D printing, where
printing hollow objects is not only possible but very common. For this reason the material cost function
is not a function of volume, but rather a function of the surface area of the supports.
Algorithm \ref{alg:material_cost} shows exactly how the cost is calculated. In here we are approximating
every beam as a cylinder, and calculating the lateral area of the cylinder.
This is a rough approximation, as there might be some overestimation in areas where beams join together,
and not all beams are cylinders (specifically the ones that connect a contact point to the support are
more similar to a truncated cone) but this approximation is good enough, as we only need to use it
as a cost function.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Support Structure Material Cost}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $F$ The rigid frame
    \Ensure $c$ The total material cost

    \Procedure{MaterialCost}{$F$}
        \State $A \gets 0$
        \For{$(a, b) \in \Call{Beams}{F}$} \Comment{Iterate over every beam once}
            \State $\ell \gets \Call{Distance}{a, b}$ \Comment{Length of the beam}
            \State $r \gets (r_a + r_b) / 2$ \Comment{Average radius of the two endpoints}
            \State $A \gets A + 2\pi r \ell$ \Comment{Lateral cylinder surface}
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $K_{area} \cdot A$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:material_cost}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Collision Cost}

The last undesirable feature of a support structure is collisions with the printed object.
This is undesirable primarily because it results in a longer cleanup phase when removing
the supports. Furthermore supports that originate from the printed object rather than the plate
are either fragile and risk detaching while the print is ongoing, or if they are 
strong enough they often are hard to remove potentially causing damage to the printed object
while being detached.
For this reason we apply a cost function that penalizes supports that intersect with the volume
of the printed object. Algorithm \ref{alg:collision_cost} shows the formulation of the cost function;
we can see that it is based on a simple iteration over the beams of the frame.
Each beam is converted into a set of equally spaced points, and a penalty is applied
for every point that is inside the volume of the printed object (plus a small safety
margin $d_{\text{exp}})$.
The function ``IsInside'' is implemented using a ``VDB'' data structure 
from the famous 2013 paper ``VDB: High-Resolution Sparse Volumes with Dynamic Topology''
\cite{openvdb}. This data structure ensures collision detection could be performed in a computationally
efficient manner ensuring the genetic algorithm could run in a reasonable time.
The implementation of the data structure is based on the rust library ``baby-shark'' \cite{babyshark}
with a few minor modifications to expose the correct API.


\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Support Structure Collision Cost}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $F$ The rigid frame
    \Require $V$ The volume occupied by the printed object
    \Ensure $c$ The total collision cost

    \Procedure{CollisionCost}{$F, V$}
        \State $V \gets \Call{Expand}{V, d_{\text{exp}}}$ \Comment{Expand the volume by $d_{\text{exp}}$ as a safety margin}
        \State $c \gets 0$
        \For{$(a, b) \in \Call{Beams}{F}$} \Comment{Iterate over every beam once}
            \State $P \gets \Call{Sample}{a, b, \delta}$ \Comment{Sample the beam every $\delta$ units}
            \For{$p \in P$}
                \If{$\Call{IsInside}{V, p}$} \Comment{The sample lies inside the printed object}
                    \State $c \gets c + K_{coll} \cdot \delta$
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $c$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:collision_cost}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Mutations}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_mut}

Thanks to the genome representation of the frame being relatively simple we are able to
define some simple yet effective fundamental mutations. Then to perform a complex mutation
we select a number $n$ of fundamental mutations we want to apply (in the range 1 to 3 in our case)
and apply the $n$ fundamental mutations in series.
The 5 fundamental mutations we defined are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AddPoint}: This simply adds a new point to the genome. To generate the point
    we randomly select the number of neighbors in the range 1 to 3, and we randomly select the position
    in a way that the $z$ value is between zero and the highest $z$ value of all the contact points, and the $x$ and $y$ values
    are generated so that the projection of the point on to the $xy$ plane falls within the
    convex hull of all contact points projected on the same $xy$ plane.
    The resulting volume where new points can be sampled from is shown in figure \ref{fig:add_point_mutation}.
    \item \textbf{MovePoint}: This mutation randomly selects one point, and moves it in a random direction
    (with a mutation that is a simple multivariate normal distribution with a mean vector of zero, and a diagonal
    covariance matrix with a fixed variance for all directions).
    \item \textbf{MovePoints}: This mutation tries to move every point in the genome, however points are moved only
    if a (biased) coin flip is successful.
    \item \textbf{MutateContacts}: This mutation mutates the number of contact points a randomly selected 
    node has, by either adding or subtracting, and then clipping the value if it ends up outside the 
    predefined range.
    \item \textbf{RemovePoint}: This mutation selects a random point and removes it from the genome.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crossover}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_cross}

Crossover is achieved in a similar way to the crossover of the contact points optimization stage (\ref{sec:contact_points_opt}).
A plane in 3D space is generated, and then a new offspring is generated by combining all the points from ``Parent A''
that fall on one side of the plane with all the points from ``Parent B'' that fall on the other side of the plane 
(as previously shown in figure \ref{fig:crossover_cpo}).
The generation of the plane is also done in the same way as the previous crossover, with the three source points being 
randomly selected from the points inside the support structure.

\subsection{Initialization}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_init}

The initialization of a fresh genome is achieved in three steps: initially we calculate the total volume
of the bounded region where points are allowed to be added (shown in orange in figure \ref{fig:add_point_mutation})
then we can calculate the amount of points our gene should have by multiplying the volume by a ``support density'' 
parameter, and then finally we can start with an empty genome, and apply the mutation ``AddPoint'' as many times as is 
necessary to reach the desired number of contact points.

\subsection{Optimization Process and Parameters}
\label{sec:sup_struct_opt_par}

The optimization process is composed of at most 5000 generations,
each of them having 100 individuals. Patience is used as an early termination strategy to avoid wasting
CPU cycles, and the maximum number of generations allowed without progress is set to 150.
The selection process is elitist, with the 10 best individuals always passing to the next generation,
while the selection of the remaining 90 individuals is done using tournament selection with a 
tournament size of 10.

\section{Exporting}
\label{sec:exporting}

The last stage in the pipeline aims to export all the support structure we generated (that 
until now is simply a graph of connected points in 3D space) into a format that could
be used to print something in the real world.
The easiest way to do so is to export the support as a 3D volume (in our case using the 
STL format), so that printing an object using the generated support was as simple as
importing the two models together in the slicer, and generating the g-code.
This was not the only option, as a different one could have been to directly integrate with 
a slicing software, which would provide some quality and usability benefits as we will discuss
in the future work section (\ref{sec:future_work}).
For this thesis we chose the first option, of exporting directly a 3D model,
both for its simpler implementation, and also for the fact that it makes the solution
more portable (as it could work with any slicing software, rather than being locked down 
to a single one).
The creation of the final volume is obtained by means of merging a set of primitive
shapes into the final structure. The shapes in question are: a cylinder that is used to
represent every beam, a cone that is used for the connection to the contact points (given that
contact points may have a radius different from the one of the beams), a sphere placed
at every intersection between multiple beams, and a ``GroundFeet'' shape (basically a short
and wide cylinder) that is added to the nodes that are attached to the ground in order 
to help them stick to the plate.
Algorithm \ref{alg:support_structure_exporting} shows the exact way that those primitive shapes
are combined into the final structure, and figure \ref{fig:export_output} shows the final
output of the exporting stage.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Creation of the final support volume}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $F$ The rigid frame
    \Require $V$ The printed object volume
    \Ensure $S$ The volume of supports

    \Procedure{ExportSupport}{$F, V$}
        \State $S \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Initialize empty support volume}
        \For{$(a, b) \in \Call{Beams}{F}$} \Comment{Iterate over beams ($a$ is always the closest to the ground)}
            \If {$\Call{IsContact}{b}$}
                \State $S \gets \Call{AddCone}{S, a, b}$
            \Else
                \State $S \gets \Call{AddCylinder}{S, a, b}$
            \EndIf
            \If {$\Call{IsGroundNode}{a}$}
                \State $S \gets \Call{AddGroundFeet}{S, a}$
            \Else
                \State $S \gets \Call{AddSphere}{S, a}$
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State $V \gets \Call{Expand}{V, d_{\text{exp}}}$ \Comment{Expand volume by a threshold}
        \State $S \gets \Call{Subtract}{S, V}$ \Comment{Subtract main volume}
        \State \Return $S$
    \EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:support_structure_exporting}
\end{algorithm}
