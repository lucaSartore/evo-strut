\chapter*{Abstract} % no number
\label{abstract}

\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract} % add to index


Additive manufacturing technology (i.e. 3D printing) has gained substantial popularity in recent years,
both in industrial settings (where technologies such as SLA and SLS dominate)
and in the consumer space (where FDM or Fused Deposition Modelling printers are
by far the most common option due to the lower price).

The fast adoption of 3D printers mostly stems from the fact that
they allow manufacturing very complex shapes that would often be impossible to produce
using more traditional manufacturing techniques; however, although they are very flexible
in the variety of producible shapes, 3D printers still cannot construct any arbitrary
object.

The biggest challenge that prevents an object from being printed in one piece
is gravity, and the impact it has on the layer segmentation procedure.
The vast majority of 3D printers work by constructing a particular object in a layered fashion,
where every layer is constructed by ``adding'' the material in the desired places.
This of course creates some issues when a large area needs to be created
without having anything below (often referred to as ``overhangs'');
in this case the layer cannot lean on anything,
meaning that most of the time it will fall down and cause a failed print.

The most common way to deal with overhangs is through the use of supports;
a support is simply something that is printed together with a specific object,
but is only there to sustain a specific overhang region while it prints, and will
then be removed. The vast majority of support structures are not designed by hand,
but are rather automatically generated by specialized algorithms.

Supports are inherently wasteful, as they are often thrown away the moment that they are
detached from the printed object; this creates a requirement on support structure optimization
algorithms, namely the objective of producing a support that minimizes the material consumption.

One key thing to understand, however, is that support structures are not necessarily portable
across different 3D printing technologies.
Indeed, some technologies (such as for example SLA, and to a lesser extent SLS) exert minimal
forces on the object while it is being printed, meanwhile in FDM, the nozzle will always
put a bit of force onto the object it is printing while it deposits the material. This means
that printing tall and skinny structures on an FDM printer often results in them bending
and ultimately causing the print to fail.
SLA and SLS 3D printing software often find themselves designing supports with
tall and skinny structures, as the thinner a support is, the less material it consumes.
FDM software, however, tends to construct structures that are a bit thicker, as supports that are too
thin would result in them bending and/or breaking during printing.

A promising approach for reducing the material necessary to print a support is the use of
genetic algorithms; indeed, this class of algorithms often excels at complex minimization
problems, where exact solutions are too complex to find. In the literature we can find
some examples of this algorithmic approach being applied for support generation,
however all the attempts were designed to work on SLS and SLA 3D printers,
while FDM is left unaddressed by the current literature.

Our work's aim is to fill the void in the literature by creating a genetic-algorithm
based support structure optimizer that is specifically designed with FDM 3D printers
in mind. In order to achieve this objective, we began by selecting a ``topology'' of the support
structure. All of the previous literature had used tree-like structures with a fixed-size
beam; this is fine for SLA and SLS, but when testing this topology on an FDM printer
we found out that the tree wasn't stiff enough, and resulted in a failed print.
Meanwhile, open-source FDM 3D printing software is also using tree topologies,
but the thickness of the beams is greater at the bottom, making the entire structure
stable (but consuming more material).
In our algorithm we decided to use a rigid frame topology (where, unlike in a tree,
any point can have multiple connections to the ground) with fixed-size beams,
as some early testing has shown promising results both in terms of stiffness and in terms
of material usage.
With the topology selected, we then proceeded to design an algorithm that was able to
optimize this topology based on a cost function.

One key component of the cost function is stiffness, as we want to prevent beams from bending and causing
a failure. However, stiffness evaluation of a complex rigid frame is a time-consuming process, and
for our genetic algorithm to converge we need to run this evaluation process hundreds of thousands
of times. For this reason, instead of using traditional stiffness evaluation algorithms, we designed
our own faster alternative. Our algorithm is a Monte Carlo approach, as we had to sacrifice a bit of
accuracy in order to get the required speed. Our tests have shown that (for our specific use case)
our algorithm is about 400 times faster, while only losing about 4\% of accuracy when compared
to exact methods.

The genetic algorithm executes in three stages. This is done to reduce
the complexity of the problem, by separating it into three different problems.
The three problems are: selection of the contact points,
grouping of different areas together, and optimization of the support structures.
The final result is a reduction of approximately 43\% in the material usage
for supports when compared to the state-of-the-art algorithm for FDM 3D printers.
The results were validated in the real world with real print tests, and all the
models were printed successfully.

Our current approach has a few downsides, including longer printing time
and slightly worse print quality (which are caused by the design of
the contact points, and we believe can be addressed in future work),
but is overall a substantial improvement over the current state of the art.
We believe that with a few refinements and ease-of-use improvements
our algorithm could save a measurable amount of plastic if
it managed to get wide adoption.
