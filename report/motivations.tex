\section{Understanding different support topologies}
\label{sec:sup_type}

When developing an algorithm that optimizes the support structure, it is often useful
to define a ``topology'' of the support (i.e. a general structure or a set of rules
that every support must follow). In theory this is not a mandatory step, as we could
put zero constraints on the optimizer, and let it come up with solutions that are unconstrained
by any specific topology; however, in practice, when applying genetic algorithms (or other optimization techniques)
the size of the search space is something that heavily impacts the performance of the optimizer.
Search spaces that are too big are often too hard to navigate, and result in poor quality solutions.
On the other hand, with some smart assumptions that limit the search space to something more manageable,
the optimizer can work more efficiently and often find better solutions than an equivalent program with
no constraints on the topology.
This kind of search space reduction is really common in the field, and both the papers we have looked at
use this technique in one way or another. For example, Vaissier et al. 2019 \cite{VAISSIER201911}
does some pruning on a pre-generated grid instead of optimizing the structure from scratch, meanwhile
Zhang et al. 2020 \cite{ZHANG2020117} uses an L-System (i.e. a set of parameters that control the generation)
to generate the tree instead of trying to optimize over the search space of all possible trees.

This feature of optimization algorithms makes it mandatory to understand the positive and negative aspects
of various topologies before selecting a specific one for an optimizer, as the choice made here will
directly affect the quality of the final solution (perhaps even more than the optimizer itself).
For this reason we will take a look at three different topologies; this will serve us to better
understand the limitations of the previous work, and thus what the motivations behind this thesis are,
but it will also function as some valuable knowledge that helps explain the decisions and motivations
behind this thesis's proposed solution. In order to study the features of different topologies, the
following case study is presented: we generated 3 different support structures that all reached the same height of
15 \SI{}{\centi\metre}; each of them had its own topology (that can be seen in figure \ref{fig:topologies}), namely:

\begin{figure}[h]

    \caption{Overview of the three different topologies analyzed}
    \label{fig:topologies}
\end{figure}



\begin{description}
    \item [ Tree: ] This structure is formed by an individual beam with a diameter of 3mm; it reaches
    the support waiting until the last moment to branch out into five different branches to ensure
    the minimum usage of material (at the expense of structural stability of course).
    \item [ Struct: ] This structure is formed by a set of four beams with a diameter of 3mm; beams are then joined together
    by some structural elements that provide greater stability compared to the tree. The structural elements are
    designed with a 45-degree angle in order to ensure that they can be printed without requiring supports.
    \item [ Cylinder: ] This is a large cylinder that is empty on the inside to save on weight; it represents by far
    the most stable option out of the 3, and it's also the most expensive in terms of material.
\end{description}




Table \ref{tab:topologies} shows the results in terms of both printing time and material consumed.
Here we can see that (at least in this evaluation, that is not considering structural stability) the tree topology
is the clear winner, as it both prints faster and requires the least material. Second place, however,
is not clear, as the struct topology is excelling in material consumption while the cylinder topology is
excelling in print time. This is due to the fact that, since the cylinder is a single piece, the nozzle does not have
to move around as much, and can therefore complete the print faster.

Even though in theory the tree structure seems to be the clear winner, when we try to print it on a real-world FDM
3D printer the z-wobble effect (\ref{fig:wobble-test}) is so prevalent that the print results in a failure.
You can see some pictures from the printing test in figure \ref{fig:topologies_print_test}, however a much clearer video on the failure can be found on
YouTube: \url{https://youtu.be/ZPdutyBA2Yg}. Fortunately the other two topologies were able to be printed without
any issue.
It is worth pointing out that the reason why the tree topology failed is strictly correlated with the technology
selected (in this case FDM); as we explained in section \ref{sec:tang_forces}, different technologies
apply different tangential forces to the object while it's being printed. It is possible, if not likely,
that the same test-case on an SLA 3D printer would have resulted in a straightforward success.
This experiment emphasizes the fact that support structure generation algorithms are not necessarily
portable between different technologies, and therefore an algorithm that is designed for example
with an SLA printer in mind may not work at all if applied to the FDM technology.

\section{Limitations of previous work}
\label{sec:limitations}

In both the papers that we have looked at (\ref{sec:paper_1} and \ref{sec:paper_2}) the topology chosen for the support
is a tree; this choice makes total sense in the context of their work, as they were focused on SLA and SLS 3D printing
techniques; however, as we just explained, this kind of topology is not adequate for FDM 3D printing.
It is reasonable to think that this limitation could be addressed with some simple modifications to the algorithm,
but this is not necessarily true. In the case of Zhang et al. 2020 \cite{ZHANG2020117} the L-System they designed is
specifically intended to build trees, therefore any adaptation to different topologies would require a complete
rewrite of the L-System.
In the case of Vaissier et al. 2019 \cite{VAISSIER201911} an adaptation to different topologies seems more possible;
their pre-generated structure from which they start pruning is already very similar to the topology we call ``struct'',
and therefore it would be possible to prune it into something that could be successfully manufactured on an FDM printer.
However the task would still not be easy, as one major hurdle would remain: in particular the ``switch-node'' encoding
that was necessary to achieve good results is incompatible with the ``struct'' topology, therefore the ``activation''
encoding would need to be used. This is potentially a complication as by the authors' own admission the first encoding type
was instrumental in getting good results, as it significantly compressed the search space. Due to the code
not being public it is impossible to know for sure whether this would work, however it seems likely that
any successful adaptation to a different topology would require designing a third encoding type suitable for the specific
use-case.

In the case of commercial software the limitations are a lot simpler to explain.
Both Normal Supports (\ref{sec:normal_supports}) and Tree Supports (\ref{sec:tree_support}) are simple ``greedy''
algorithms that don't perform any kind of optimization. This is not necessarily a bad thing, as greedy solutions are
extremely fast, and can often be quite good if done correctly. However, the question of how much waste is being generated
by not performing any sort of optimization still remains, and answering such a question is one of the key contributions
of this thesis.



\section{Stated objective}
\label{sec:obj}

All the previous academic literature that we have looked at in section \ref{sec:acc_lit} is primarily
focused on industrial technologies such as SLS printing; this means that their work is not easily
applicable to FDM 3D printing due to the limitations that we explained in section \ref{sec:limitations}.
On the other hand, commercial tools available in slicers such as Cura and OrcaSlicer are obviously compatible
with the FDM technology, but are not subject to any (sophisticated) optimization.
The objective of this thesis is to bridge the gap between these two worlds, and to create the first public
support structure optimizer based on genetic algorithms that is created from the ground up to work with
FDM 3D printers, in order to answer our primary research question:
``How much material can be saved by applying genetic algorithms to support structure optimization in FDM 3D printing?''

\subsection{Action plan}
In order to achieve the objectives, there are three separate steps that we need to take:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Selecting a topology for the supports
    \item Creating an algorithm that is able to evaluate stiffness in the solution space defined by the topology selected
    \item Identifying a suitable encoding for a solution and developing an optimization algorithm that works with an arbitrary cost function.
\end{itemize}
The first step is the most straightforward one, as we already explored various topologies in section \ref{sec:sup_type}.
Having seen the results it is easy to exclude the ``tree'' topology as it can result in structures that are impossible to print.
The two remaining options are thus ``struct'' and ``cylinder''. Looking at table \ref{tab:topologies} it is clear that the struct
is the most promising in terms of material used, while the cylinder is more promising in terms of print time. Since the material reduction
is often a more important objective than time reduction, and since the difference in material is much bigger than the difference
in time, the choice of the ``struct'' topology becomes obvious.
The last two steps are more convoluted than the simple choice of topology, and are explained in chapters \ref{cha:stiffness_algo}
and \ref{cha:support_opt_alg}.
